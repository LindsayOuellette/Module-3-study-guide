    The thread running through all of it

    One study hands you one result and no way to check it. Everything in this module — effect sizes, power, error, replication, intervals — is a tool for being honest about how much that one result is worth.

    Part 1 · t-tests

    Which test, and why

    Every t-test compares means. What changes is what you are comparing them to.

    One sample

    Compares your one group against a fixed, known value — a national average, a published norm, a recommended amount.

    H0: no difference between our group and the comparison value.

    Independent samples

    Compares two separate groups of different people.

    aka between groups · between subjects · unpaired

    H0: no difference between the two groups.

    Paired samples

    Compares the same people twice — before and after, or in two conditions.

    aka within group · within subjects · dependent samples

    H0: no difference between Time 1 and Time 2 (or Condition 1 and Condition 2).

    The one idea that covers all three

    The null hypothesis always starts with “no difference.” Only the thing you are comparing changes — a fixed value, another group, or the same people at a second time point.

    The question that decides it

    Who provided the scores?

    • One group, compared to a number → one sample
    • Two different sets of people → independent
    • The same people, measured twice → paired

    A note on the paired test

    Strictly, the paired null says the mean of the difference scores is zero — not simply that the two means are equal. You subtract each person's score at Time 1 from their score at Time 2 first, then ask whether those differences average out to nothing.

    Where everything lives

    You are not calculating anything. You are finding numbers and putting them in the right places.

    Every t-test output has the same three parts, in the same order. Once you can see the shape, the rest is just reading.

    1 · The test line

    t, df and p-value, all on one line, right under the name of the test. The name tells you which t-test was run.

    2 · sample estimates

    The means. One for a one sample test, two for the others. These are not the standard deviations.

    3 · The describe table

    Everything else — n, sd, median, min, max. The only columns you need for an APA write-up are n and sd.

    The order you fill things in

    Means and SDs first, because they come in pairs and are easy to mix up. Then t, df and p off the test line. Then the effect size. Then decide: is p below .05?

    The APA sentence, in pieces

    The shape every write-up follows

    Which test was run and what it compared. Which group was higher, with both means and SDs. t(df) = t, p = p, d = d.

    What actually has to be in there

    Required

    • The mean and standard deviation for each group
    • t, with df in parentheses
    • The p value
    • Which group was higher — in words

    Recommended, not required

    • Cohen's d
    • The confidence interval

    APA encourages both, and good papers include them — but a write-up is not wrong for leaving them out. Include the effect size in this class.

    Say what happened, not what the software did

    “Students who studied in silence recalled significantly more words” — not “the t-test was significant.” Name the groups and the direction. A reader should be able to follow your sentence without seeing the output.

    Part 3 · Effect size

    Significance is only half the story

    p answers “is it there?” — d answers “is it big?”

    What p tells you

    Whether the difference you found is big enough, relative to the noise, to be taken seriously. Below .05, you reject the null.

    It says nothing about size. A trivial difference can be highly significant, and a large difference can come back non-significant.

    What Cohen's d tells you

    How far apart the two means are, measured in standard deviations. It is the size of the difference, and it does not depend on how many people you tested.

    .2 small · .5 medium · .8 large.

    The four combinations

    Significant + big d

    Real, and worth acting on. The clean case.

    Significant + tiny d

    Real, and probably not worth acting on. Easy to oversell.

    Not significant + medium or big d

    Unresolved. A likely clue the study was too small. Never report this as “no effect”.

    Not significant + tiny d

    Both numbers agree there is nothing here. A useful finding.

    Say this out loud until it sticks

    “Not significant” is not the same as “no difference.” It means this study could not rule out zero — which is a statement about the study as much as about the world.

    Part 4 · Power

    Why we run a power analysis

    Power is the chance your study will find the effect, if the effect is really there.

    Everything else in this module is about interpreting a result you already have. Power is the one thing you control in advance. Before collecting a single data point, you are trying to set the study up so that if there is something to find, you will find it. That is all power is: giving yourself the best possible chance of a significant result.

    Two things drive it

    How big the effect is. Big effects are easy to spot.

    How many people you test. The part you actually control.

    You cannot change the effect out in the world. You can always change the sample size.

    The target is 80%

    At 80% power, a real effect of the size you expect shows up as significant in about 80 of every 100 studies — and gets missed in 20. Those 20 are Type II errors, and from inside any one of them you could not tell.

    The question this answers

    Non-significant result, but a decent effect size?

    That is a clue the study was underpowered — not evidence that nothing is there. A medium or large d that comes back p > .05 is the signature of a study with too few people. It is also exactly why we run a power analysis before collecting data: so we never end up in that position.

    Designing a study

    “I expect a small effect.” Then you need roughly 400 per group. If you can only recruit 40, you already know the study will probably fail — before you start. That is the whole value of a power analysis.

    Judging someone else's study

    “They tested 20 per group and found nothing.” With 20 per group you only have a good chance of catching enormous effects. So their non-significant result rules out almost nothing. Small study + null result = no information.

    Part 5 · Decision error

    The two ways to be wrong

    Every time you decide about a null hypothesis, reality has already settled the matter. You just can't see it.

    The null is actually TRUE
    (no real difference)    		The null is actually FALSE
    (there is a real difference)
    You reject
    p < .05    		Type I error
    A false positive. You claimed something that isn't there.    		Correct
    You found a real effect.
    You fail to reject
    p ≥ .05    		Correct
    You correctly found nothing.    		Type II error
    A false negative. You missed something real.

    Type I · false positive

    Crying wolf when there is no wolf.

    You set this rate yourself when you choose .05 — you are agreeing, up front, to be wrong this way 5% of the time when the null is true.

    Type II · false negative

    Missing the wolf that is actually there.

    You don't set this one directly. It falls out of how big the effect is and how many people you tested — which is to say, out of your power.

    The part that costs marks

    Both errors are defined by what reality was doing, not by anything the researcher did wrong. A perfectly designed study still produces false positives 5% of the time when the null is true. It is the price of admission, not a mistake.

    Part 5 · Decision error

    You cannot know from one study

    Which is why one study is never the answer.

    Here is the uncomfortable fact underneath this whole module. You run your study, you get p = .03, you reject the null. Did you find something real, or did you get a lucky sample? There is no test for that. The only thing that separates a real effect from a fluke is whether it shows up again.

    Error rates describe the long run

    “Wrong 5% of the time” describes what happens across many studies. It says nothing about whether your result is one of the wrong ones. A single p value cannot be interrogated.

    Replication is the test

    If an effect is real, independent researchers with fresh samples keep finding it. If it was a lucky sample, it evaporates. Repetition is the only instrument we have.

    Go back and run the twelve-studies simulator again

    Set it to a small effect and a small sample. Every one of those twelve studies is investigating a real effect, and most of them miss it. Now imagine only the significant ones get published — the literature would look unanimous, built entirely out of the handful that got lucky. That is the replication crisis in one picture.

    Part 6 · Confidence intervals

    What your sample says about the population

    You measured a sample. The thing you actually care about is the population.

    Your study gives you one number — a mean, or a difference between two means. That number is almost certainly not the true population value, because you only looked at a sample. A confidence interval answers the obvious next question: given what I found, what is the true population value likely to be?

    Read an interval like this

    “95% CI [2.1, 8.4]” means: we are confident the true population value lies somewhere between 2.1 and 8.4. It is not the range of your data and not the range of your participants' scores. It is a range of believable truths about the population.

    Width is precision

    Two researchers can both be telling you the truth and still be giving you wildly different amounts of information. Imagine asking two friends when they will arrive. One says “sometime between noon and 8pm.” The other says “between 2:45 and 3:15.” Neither is lying — but only one of them lets you plan your afternoon.

    Three studies estimating the same thingSame estimate, very different precision

    All three intervals are centred in the same place, so all three point to roughly the same answer. But Study A is consistent with almost anything, while Study C has pinned the population value down. A narrower interval is a more precise estimate — and that is what we want.

    A wide interval

    You have a vague answer. The population value could plausibly be near the bottom of the range or near the top, and those might mean very different things in practice.

    A narrow interval

    You have pinned the population value down. You can say not just that there is an effect but roughly how big it is.

    Why the interval is worth reporting

    A p value tells you whether to reject the null. The interval tells you what the population value probably is — which is usually the thing you actually wanted to know.

    The APA line

    Memorise the shape

    Which test and what it compared. Which group was higher, with M and SD for each. Then t(df) = __, p = __, d = __.

    If you remember three things

    1. “Not significant” means this study couldn't tell — not that nothing is there. 2. Always read p and d together; a decent d with a non-significant p is a clue the study was underpowered. 3. You can never know whether you made an error, which is why nothing counts until it replicates.
